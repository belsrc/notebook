## Why linear chain-of-thought has a structural problem

Chain-of-Thought prompting asks the model to produce intermediate reasoning tokens before the final answer. It works because (a) those tokens give the model more compute, and (b) the autoregressive distribution $p_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x)$ conditions later tokens on earlier ones, so partial work biases the rest of the generation toward consistency with it.

That last property is also the failure mode. Once a token is generated, every token after it is conditioned on that token. The model has no mechanism to:

1. consider several alternative next steps and pick the best one,
2. evaluate whether the current step is promising,
3. undo a step that turned out to be a dead end.

A bad early step doesn't get corrected. It gets committed to and elaborated on.

Self-consistency is a partial workaround: sample $n$ independent chains and majority-vote the final answers. It helps, but each chain is still internally linear, and the only "evaluator" is the final-answer vote. There is no per-step assessment. You're averaging over many flawed walks rather than fixing the walk.

Consider Game of 24: given four numbers, combine them with $+, -, \times, \div$ (each number used once) to reach 24. GPT-4 with CoT solves about 4% of instances; with ToT it solves 74%. The gap is enormous, and it isn't because the model "doesn't know arithmetic." It does. The problem is that the first arithmetic step the model produces is often a bad one, and CoT can't recover from it.

The fix is to give the model a structure that allows exploration, evaluation, and revision. That structure is a tree.

## The conceptual shift: thoughts as nodes

Define a **thought** as a coherent intermediate reasoning step, bigger than a token, smaller than a complete answer. In Game of 24, a thought is one arithmetic operation: "10 − 4 = 6 (remaining: 6, 9, 13)." In a creative-writing task, a thought might be a paragraph-level plan. In code generation, a thought might be a function signature plus a one-line description of its behavior.

A **state** is the input plus the sequence of thoughts so far. Two thoughts produce two distinct states.

If we treat states as nodes and "appending a next thought" as an edge, the model's reasoning becomes a search tree:

```
                       s_0  (problem)
                     /  |  \
                   /    |    \
                 s_a   s_b   s_c       ← generator produced k candidate thoughts
                / \     |
              s_aa s_ab s_b1            ← each evaluated; weak branches pruned
              ...
              s* (a state where the goal test passes)
```

This is a move from generating tokens to searching over states. Once we make that move, classical search algorithms apply directly.

## The Tree of Thoughts framework: four components

Yao et al. (2023) define ToT through four pieces. Holding these distinct is the single most important thing in this module, because each piece is a design decision you make independently.

Let $S$ be the space of partial reasoning states, $s_0 \in S$ the initial problem, and $p_\theta$ the language model.

**Component 1: Thought decomposition.** Decide what a thought *is* for this task. This sets the branching granularity. Too coarse (a thought = the whole answer) collapses ToT into CoT-SC. Too fine (a thought = one token) is intractable. Yao et al. recommend a level at which a human could plausibly judge whether the step is on the right track.

**Component 2: Thought generator $G(p_\theta, s, k)$.** Given a state $s$, produce $k$ candidate next-thoughts. Two strategies:

- *Sample*: draw $k$ i.i.d. samples from $p_\theta(\text{thought} \mid s)$. Works when the space of valid continuations is broad, like creative writing where many distinct continuations are plausible.
- *Propose*: prompt the model to enumerate $k$ distinct candidates in one call ("propose 5 different next steps"). Works when the space is narrow (Game of 24 has only a handful of valid arithmetic combinations; i.i.d. sampling produces duplicates).

**Component 3: State evaluator $V(p_\theta, S')$.** Score each candidate state. This is the heuristic that drives the search. Two strategies:

- *Value*: rate each state independently. "Given the remaining numbers, is 24 reachable? Output sure / likely / impossible." Map to a numeric score. Cheap, parallelizable, suffers from miscalibrated absolute judgments.
- *Vote*: compare candidates against each other. "Which of these 5 drafts is most promising? Output one." Aggregate by counting. More reliable when relative comparison is easier than absolute judgment, which is typical for open-ended tasks.

**Component 4: Search algorithm.** Combine generator and evaluator into a tree traversal: BFS, DFS, beam search, best-first, or (in extensions) Monte Carlo Tree Search.

Holding these four pieces apart matters because each can be swapped without touching the others. You can keep the generator and evaluator fixed and change BFS to DFS to see what happens. You can keep the search fixed and change vote to value to see how it changes the answer. The original ToT paper uses BFS for Game of 24 and Mini Crosswords, DFS for Creative Writing.

## Classical search: the connection

Because ToT *is* tree search, the classical formalism applies directly. A search problem is the tuple:

$$
\langle S, A, T, s_0, \text{Goal} \rangle
$$

where $S$ is the state space, $A$ are actions (here: candidate thoughts), $T: S \times A \to S$ is the transition function (here: append thought to state), $s_0$ is the initial problem, and $\text{Goal}: S \to \{\text{true, false}\}$ tests whether the answer is reached.

The four algorithms below differ only in how they order the frontier:

| Algorithm      | Frontier data structure    | Behavior                            |
| -------------- | -------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| BFS            | FIFO queue                 | Shallowest first; level-by-level    |
| DFS            | LIFO stack                 | Deepest first; natural backtracking |
| Best-first     | Priority queue on `h(s)`   | Heuristic-guided; greedy            |
| A*             | Priority queue `g(s)+h(s)` | Optimal if h admissible             |
| Beam (width w) | Top-w of each level by h   | Like BFS but pruned; not complete   |

In the ToT mapping:

1. The evaluator $V$ plays the role of the heuristic $h$.
2. There is typically no notion of $g(s)$ (cost-so-far), so plain A* doesn't directly apply.
3. BFS-ToT with a beam width $b$ at each level is exactly **beam search**: at depth $d$, evaluate all expansions of the surviving $b$ parents, keep the top $b$ children, advance.

This is worth pausing on. The phrase "BFS with beam width" might sound like a tweak, but it is the entire algorithm Yao et al. use for Game of 24. ToT does not propose a new search algorithm. It proposes to use search at all, with an LM as both the action generator and the heuristic. The novelty is the framing, not the algorithm.

## Worked example: Game of 24 (BFS variant)

The full setup from the paper:

- *Task*: Given four integers, combine with $+, -, \times, \div$ (each number used once) to make 24.
- *Decomposition*: a thought is one arithmetic operation reducing the count of remaining numbers by one. Three thoughts take us from four numbers to one.
- *Generator*: propose mode, $k = 8$ candidate moves per state.
- *Evaluator*: value mode. For each resulting state of remaining numbers, prompt the LM: "Given these numbers, can you reach 24? Answer sure / likely / impossible." Map to scores (e.g., sure = 20, likely = 1, impossible = 0.001).
- *Search*: BFS with beam width $b = 5$, depth $d = 3$.

A single expansion looks like this for input (4, 9, 10, 13):

```
                       (4, 9, 10, 13)                    depth 0
                              │
                       propose 8 moves
                              │
       ┌──────────────┬───────┴──────┬──────────────┐
       │              │              │              │
   13 − 9 = 4     10 − 4 = 6     4 + 9 = 13     10 × 4 = 40   ...
   → (4, 4, 10)   → (6, 9, 13)   → (10, 13, 13)  → (9, 13, 40)
   V = likely     V = sure       V = likely      V = impossible

   Keep top 5 children by V.
   Repeat the same propose/evaluate cycle for each survivor.
                              │
                          depth 1
                              │
                          depth 2
                              │
                          depth 3: one number remains; goal-test == 24
```

Notice what just happened: the model never had to commit to a first move that came to mind. The bad branch ($10 \times 4 = 40$, which puts us above 24 with three numbers left) gets scored "impossible" and pruned after one step. That's the structural difference from CoT: wrong steps are dropped instead of elaborated on.

The fact that an "impossible" evaluation is itself just another LM call is something to flag now and come back to in §8. It works *because* judging "can these numbers reach 24" is much easier than producing the arithmetic; the model can verify what it can't always generate.

## Worked example: Creative Writing (DFS variant)

The Creative Writing task in the paper: generate a coherent four-paragraph passage whose four paragraphs each end with a pre-specified sentence. The constraint forces the model to plan, because random local generation almost never lands the prescribed endings naturally.

Setup:

- *Decomposition*: two levels. First, a high-level plan (one thought per plan). Then, a full passage written according to a chosen plan (one thought per passage).
- *Generator*: sample mode, $k = 5$. The space is open-ended, so sampling is the right move.
- *Evaluator*: vote mode. Show all five candidates to the model and ask which is best. Aggregate by counting.
- *Search*: DFS. Generate plans, vote best, expand into passages, vote best, return. Depth is 2.

DFS is the right shape here because the tree is shallow and the branching is "creative." There's no need to exhaustively explore the level, just commit to a promising path and fall back only if it doesn't pan out.

## What "backtracking" means for an LLM

In classical AI, backtracking is mechanical: pop the stack and restore the previous variable bindings. For an LLM, the state of the world is the **prompt context**. So "backtracking" really means that when you generate the next branch, you don't include the failed branch's thoughts in the prompt.

Two implementation styles:

**Style A: stateless reconstruction (recommended).** Each generation call assembles its prompt from scratch as `(root_problem) ++ (path_of_thoughts_to_this_node)`. Sibling branches never see each other; the LM is treated as a stateless function $p_\theta(\cdot \mid \text{prompt})$. This is the standard ToT implementation. It is clean, parallelizable, and easy to reason about. There's no hidden bias from sibling branches, and children of the same parent can be expanded concurrently.

**Style B: conversational backtrack.** In a chat-style implementation you can say "Forget the previous attempt; try a different approach." This is fragile. The model has seen the failed branch and the negative phrasing, which biases the next generation. Avoid this in favor of Style A.

The implication is that a real ToT framework owns prompt construction. It does not delegate "memory" to the LM; it explicitly assembles the relevant path each call. This is the same discipline that distinguishes a stateless tool call from a chat-style integration in agent design generally.

## The evaluator is the bottleneck: caveats

The whole tree search rises or falls on the evaluator. If $V$ is uncorrelated with "actually solves the task," ToT degenerates into random tree expansion at $O(b^d)$ cost. Here is how LM-as-judge fails in practice:

1. *Absolute scoring drift*. Asking "rate this 1 to 10" gives noisy, miscalibrated scores. The same state scored twice can get different numbers.
2. *Position bias in voting*. In pairwise votes, the option presented first is systematically favored. Shuffling order mitigates.
3. *Self-favoring*. An LM evaluating its own outputs tends to score them higher than identical outputs framed as coming from elsewhere.
4. *Confidence ≠ correctness*. A "sure" verdict is a token distribution, not a guarantee, especially on hard problems where the model is also wrong at the underlying task.

Practical mitigations: shuffle option order, take majority over multiple evaluations, and use a different model (or a smaller specialist model) for evaluation than for generation. Most important: replace the LM evaluator with a deterministic check whenever one exists.

For Game of 24, arithmetic is cheap and reliable; the LM only needs to propose, not verify. The same logic applies to code (run a unit test), SQL (parse and execute), or symbolic math (simplify and compare). Substituting a deterministic verifier for an LM evaluator removes the largest single source of variance in the whole pipeline.

Anthropic's *Building Effective Agents* (2024) makes the same point in a more general form: agents work well when "tasks have clear success criteria and verifiable outputs". The verifier doesn't need to be an LM. Often it shouldn't be.

## A reference implementation

A minimal BFS-ToT in TypeScript. Stateless reconstruction, parallel expansion, batched evaluation. Following safety > performance > simplicity > readability:

```typescript
type Thought = string;

type State = {
  readonly problem: string;
  readonly path: ReadonlyArray<Thought>;
};

type Score = number;

type Evaluated = {
  readonly state: State;
  readonly score: Score;
};

type ToTConfig = {
  readonly maxDepth: number;        // d: maximum search depth
  readonly branchingFactor: number; // k: candidates per expansion
  readonly beamWidth: number;       // b: survivors per level
  readonly generate: (s: State, k: number) => Promise<ReadonlyArray<Thought>>;
  readonly evaluate: (states: ReadonlyArray<State>) => Promise<ReadonlyArray<Score>>;
  readonly isGoal: (s: State) => boolean;
};

const extend = (s: State, t: Thought): State => ({
  problem: s.problem,
  path: [...s.path, t],
});

const expandFrontier = async (
  frontier: ReadonlyArray<State>,
  cfg: ToTConfig,
): Promise<ReadonlyArray<State>> => {
  // I/O-bound LM calls. Parallelize across the frontier.
  const groups = await Promise.all(
    frontier.map(async (s) => {
      const ts = await cfg.generate(s, cfg.branchingFactor);
      return ts.map((t) => extend(s, t));
    }),
  );
  return groups.flat();
};

const selectTopB = (
  evaluated: ReadonlyArray<Evaluated>,
  b: number,
): ReadonlyArray<State> => {
  // Stable sort by score descending, slice top b.
  const sorted = [...evaluated].sort((a, z) => z.score - a.score);
  return sorted.slice(0, b).map((e) => e.state);
};

const treeOfThoughtsBFS = async (
  problem: string,
  cfg: ToTConfig,
): Promise<State | null> => {
  const root: State = { problem, path: [] };

  const step = async (
    depth: number,
    frontier: ReadonlyArray<State>,
  ): Promise<State | null> => {
    if (frontier.length === 0) return null;

    const hit = frontier.find(cfg.isGoal);
    if (hit !== undefined) return hit;

    if (depth >= cfg.maxDepth) return null;

    const children = await expandFrontier(frontier, cfg);
    if (children.length === 0) return null;

    const scores = await cfg.evaluate(children);
    const evaluated: ReadonlyArray<Evaluated> = children.map((s, i) => ({
      state: s,
      score: scores[i] ?? 0,
    }));

    const survivors = selectTopB(evaluated, cfg.beamWidth);
    return step(depth + 1, survivors);
  };

  return step(0, [root]);
};
```

A few design points to internalize:

1. `State` is immutable. Branching creates a fresh structure rather than mutating shared state. This rules out a class of cross-branch contamination bugs that are otherwise easy to introduce when paths converge in memory.
2. `expandFrontier` uses `Promise.all` because generation calls are I/O-bound on the LM. Children of different parents are completely independent and can be in flight simultaneously.
3. `evaluate` takes a batch of states because vote-mode evaluation needs to compare them. Even in value mode you want batching for throughput.
4. The recursion depth is bounded by `maxDepth`, so the stack is fine. If `maxDepth` were unbounded you'd rewrite as a `while`-loop rebinding a frontier reference.

The DFS variant differs only in exploration order: pick the best child, recurse, fall through to the next-best sibling if the recursion returns null. Backtracking is the natural fall-through of the for-loop:

```typescript
const treeOfThoughtsDFS = async (
  problem: string,
  cfg: ToTConfig,
): Promise<State | null> => {
  const root: State = { problem, path: [] };

  const visit = async (s: State, depth: number): Promise<State | null> => {
    if (cfg.isGoal(s)) return s;
    if (depth >= cfg.maxDepth) return null;

    const ts = await cfg.generate(s, cfg.branchingFactor);
    const children = ts.map((t) => extend(s, t));
    const scores = await cfg.evaluate(children);

    const ranked = children
      .map((c, i) => ({ c, score: scores[i] ?? 0 }))
      .sort((a, z) => z.score - a.score);

    for (const { c } of ranked) {
      const found = await visit(c, depth + 1);
      if (found !== null) return found;
      // Implicit backtrack: loop continues to the next-best sibling.
    }
    return null;
  };

  return visit(root, 0);
};
```

This is the same structure as classical recursive backtracking used for N-Queens placement, Sudoku solvers, or Prolog-style resolution. The only thing that changed is what generates the candidates (an LM instead of an enumeration of legal moves) and what scores them (an LM evaluator instead of a domain function).

## Cost analysis

Be honest about cost. With branching factor $k$, beam width $b$, depth $d$, one generation call per parent expansion (producing $k$ candidates in one call via the propose strategy), and one evaluation call per child:

$$
\text{LM calls} \;\approx\; \underbrace{1}_{\text{root}} \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{d} \Big( \underbrace{b}_{\text{parents}} \;+\; \underbrace{b \cdot k}_{\text{children, if eval-per-child}} \Big) \;=\; O\!\left( d \cdot b \cdot k \right)
$$

If you batch evaluation into one call per level (vote mode), the $b \cdot k$ term collapses to $b$ per level, but you still pay the generation cost.

Concretely, for Game of 24 with $b = 5$, $k = 8$, $d = 3$: about $3 \times 5 = 15$ generation calls and $3 \times 5 \times 8 = 120$ evaluation calls. Call it roughly $130\times$ a single CoT pass, give or take batching. CoT-SC with $n = 100$ samples runs at $100\times$. Same order of magnitude as heavy CoT-SC, with substantially better accuracy on hard reasoning.

The question is whether the per-query value justifies $\sim 100\times$ token cost relative to a plain CoT pass. For an offline scientific computation, easily yes. For an interactive chatbot reply, probably not.

## Comparison to neighboring methods

| Method    | Structure            | Branching  | Backtrack     | Evaluator               |
| --------- | -------------------- | ---------- | ------------- | ----------------------- |
| CoT       | single linear chain  | none       | no            | none                    |
| CoT-SC    | n indep. chains      | none per   | no            | majority vote at end    |
| ReAct     | linear trace + tools | none       | no (linear)   | env. observations       |
| Reflexion | multi-trial, linear  | none per   | across trials | external task signal    |
| ToT       | tree                 | k per node | within search | LM-as-judge OR external |
| GoT       | DAG (merging)        | yes        | yes + merge   | LM-as-judge             |
| LATS      | tree with MCTS       | yes        | yes + UCB1    | LM + environment        |

Two grouping observations to take away.

**Linear vs. structured.** CoT, CoT-SC, ReAct, and Reflexion are all fundamentally linear at the per-attempt level. Reflexion adds a meta-loop across attempts and writes verbal critiques into an episodic memory, but each attempt is still a single trajectory. ToT is the first method on the list that lets a single attempt branch and recombine.

**Open-loop vs. closed-loop evaluation.** ReAct gets feedback from a real environment (search results, tool outputs, sensor readings). ToT, by default, gets feedback from the LM itself judging intermediate states. The first is generally more reliable but is only available when there is an environment to query. The line between them blurs: a ToT setup that calls an external verifier as the evaluator (a deterministic arithmetic check, a code unit test, a SQL execution) is essentially using closed-loop evaluation inside a tree-search policy.

The most useful framing: **self-consistency is a degenerate ToT of depth 1 and width $n$ with a majority-vote evaluator.** Once you see that, the methods stop looking like a list of named techniques and start looking like points on the same design space:

$$
\text{Design space} = (\text{topology}, \text{generation strategy}, \text{evaluation strategy})
$$

## When NOT to use Tree of Thoughts

Anthropic's *Building Effective Agents* (2024) argues, correctly in my view, that engineers reach for elaborate agent patterns much too quickly. The recommended progression goes from simple to complex, and you stop at the first level that solves the problem:

1. A single well-prompted LM call.
2. Prompt chaining (linear handoffs).
3. Routing (classify, dispatch).
4. Parallelization (sectioning or voting; CoT-SC lives here).
5. Orchestrator-workers (a planner delegates subtasks).
6. Evaluator-optimizer (generate, critique, revise in a tight loop).
7. Autonomous agents (tool-using, self-directing).

ToT is closest to a parameterized form of evaluator-optimizer with tree-structured generation. It belongs near the top of the hierarchy. The cost-benefit is favorable when:

1. The task has well-defined intermediate state that can be evaluated.
2. A reliable evaluator exists, preferably deterministic (a unit test, arithmetic check, executable spec).
3. Failure of linear reasoning is *empirically demonstrated* on the actual task, not assumed.
4. Latency and cost budgets allow $10\times$ to $100\times$ a CoT call.

It is the wrong tool when:

1. The evaluator is "another LM call with vibes." You're paying tree-search costs for an unreliable signal, and the noise compounds at every node.
2. A single CoT or CoT-SC pass already gets you to acceptable accuracy.
3. The branching factor is large and the heuristic is weak, so search degenerates to expensive random expansion.
4. The product needs interactive latency. A 200-call tree won't return in under a second.

The strongest version of the Anthropic argument: when you find yourself reaching for ToT, first ask whether the task actually has a verifiable success signal you could give to a simpler evaluator-optimizer loop. If yes, build that first. ToT is rarely the smallest pattern that works.

## Extensions worth knowing exist

1. **Graph of Thoughts** generalizes the tree to a DAG by allowing thoughts to be merged and aggregated. Useful when partial solutions to subproblems can be combined into a solution to the whole.
2. **Language Agent Tree Search (LATS)** replaces fixed-policy ToT (BFS/DFS) with Monte Carlo Tree Search, using a UCB1-style exploration term to trade off exploration vs. exploitation, and incorporates environment feedback. This is the closest thing to AlphaGo-style reasoning over LM thoughts that has been published.
3. **Process reward models** (a parallel line of work, e.g., Lightman et al., *Let's Verify Step by Step*, ICLR 2024) train a dedicated model to score reasoning steps. A trained PRM as the evaluator $V$ is far more reliable than an LM-as-judge zero-shot, and is the practical evaluator of choice when you have the training data.

The unifying takeaway across all of these: **separate the policy (thought generation) from the value (thought evaluation), structure the interaction explicitly as a search, and pick the search algorithm and evaluator suited to your task.** Once that separation is made, you get classical search algorithms and decades of work on heuristics, pruning, and exploration for free. ToT's contribution is the framing. The algorithms it uses are old.
